# Teaching Plan Design

Confirm learners, subject, topic, curriculum or training standard, prior knowledge, lesson duration, class size, environment, available materials, accessibility needs, and evidence of learning. Use the standard version supplied by the user or verify the current authoritative version; do not claim alignment from a topic name alone.

## Design backward from learning

Write a small set of observable objectives: what learners will do, under what conditions, and to what success criterion. Avoid vague verbs such as understand unless paired with observable evidence. Map each objective to an assessment and each assessment to an activity that prepares learners for it.

Plan the lesson as a timed sequence:

1. retrieve or diagnose prerequisite knowledge;
2. establish a meaningful problem or learning purpose;
3. model or explain with examples and non-examples;
4. guide practice with checks for understanding;
5. let learners apply independently or collaboratively;
6. assess the stated objectives and close with retrieval or reflection.

Budget transitions and feedback time. Prefer a coherent learning progression over forcing every taxonomy level into one lesson.

## Make it usable for real learners

Anticipate likely misconceptions and specify how the teacher will detect and respond to them. Provide supports, extension, language scaffolds, and accessible alternatives without lowering the core objective. Use age-appropriate examples and protect student privacy.

For homework or practice, state purpose, estimated time, differentiation, and feedback method. For a rubric, use observable criteria and distinguish quality levels concretely.

Deliver:

- context and verified standards basis;
- objective–assessment–activity alignment table;
- timed teacher and learner sequence;
- key questions, examples, misconceptions, and responses;
- differentiation and accessibility plan;
- formative checks, exit evidence, and follow-up decision rules;
- materials and a pre-class readiness checklist.

Flag content that needs subject-matter review. Do not fabricate textbook passages, policy clauses, learner data, or evidence that the lesson was effective before it is taught.
